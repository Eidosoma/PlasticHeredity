# Strict-eight geometry audit — lay summary

We asked two skeptical questions about the rare strict-eight event.

First, are eight daughters called “coherent” only because the assembly has
collapsed to one dominant molecule? Usually not. Under the registered cosine
definition, the daughters contained about 12 occupied molecular types and five
to six effective species on average. Only 0.13% and 0.04% of candidate-02 and
candidate-03 events were at least 80% dominated by one type in every daughter.

That does not mean diversity is irrelevant. Compared with difficult non-event
futures from the exact same starting state that still managed an inherited run
of eight, strict events had roughly five fewer effective species, five fewer
occupied types, a leading-species share about 0.22 larger, and substantially
less compositional turnover. The event is therefore associated with a real
stability–diversity trade-off: it is not normally a one-species copy, but it is
markedly more concentrated than a comparable failed event.

Second, does the same event appear if composition is compared in a different
reasonable way? A separately calibrated Bray–Curtis definition still found
events, but only about 0.47–0.66% of futures rather than 1.84–2.10% under
cosine, and most positive futures were different. The greatest disagreement
came from deciding whether all eight daughters occupied one mutually coherent
region.

The conclusion is deliberately narrow: registered-cosine strict-eight
occurrence is real within its operational definition and not generally a
single-species artefact, but its frequency and membership depend strongly on
the similarity metric. It follows one selected daughter at each fission and is
not evidence of faithful reproduction by both daughters.
